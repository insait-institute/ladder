LADDER: A Long-Horizon Robot Manipulation Benchmark

Petko Petkov1, Giuliano Albanese1, Nikolay Nikolov1, Luc Van Gool1, Jonathan Tremblay2,†, Danda Pani Paudel1,†
1Institute for Computer Science, Artificial Intelligence and Technology 2NVIDIA †Equal supervision
Paper Code Hugging Face

Overview of our benchmark and key findings. Across all suites, orchestrating policy execution with sequential subgoal decomposition and visual verification substantially outperforms issuing the full instruction directly to the policy (bottom right). See Tables 1 and 3.

Abstract

General-purpose robot manipulation policies learn short-horizon skills effectively, but degrade rapidly on long-horizon tasks. Existing benchmarks cannot isolate failures because task horizon is either too short or entangled with scene and object variations. We propose LADDER, a long-horizon robot manipulation benchmark scaling from 1 to 10 subgoals within the same environment over three suites (SimplerEnv-Long, RobotArena-∞-Long, and RoboLab-Long), 145 tasks, eight categories, two embodiments, and 2,024 simulated episodes per system. We evaluate two orchestration paradigms: text subgoals for learned low-level policies (VoLo) and tool selection orchestration (CaP-X, GPT-Policy, PSP-Agent, TiPToP). Given the full instruction directly, low-level policies achieve 85.4% on single-subgoal tasks, but drop to near zero on extended tasks. While orchestration remedies this, it remains bounded by physical execution errors of the low-level policy rather than high-level planning and achieves only ∼25%. Tool selection orchestration with GPT-Policy on GPT-6 Astra significantly outperforms learned-policy methods, but incurs significant time and cost budget and succeeds only ∼62%. Real-world experiments across two embodiments confirm the collapse of the low-level policy and the gain from orchestration. Project website: ladderrobotics.ai

Eight task categories

Single destination 44 tasks

Every object goes to the same destination.

“put both carrots, the spoon and the eggplant on the plate”

Transfer 8 tasks

Every object starts on one destination and is moved to another.

“move the carrot and the spoon from the plate to the blue towel”

Split destination 25 tasks

Different objects go to different destinations.

“put the lemon on the plate and put the orange in the bin”

Relay 20 tasks

Objects pass through an intermediate destination.

“first put the carrot on the plate, then put the carrot on the blue towel”

Revisit 13 tasks

Objects return to a destination they have already left.

“first put the spoon on the cloth, then put the spoon in the pot, then put the spoon on the cloth”

Exclusion 13 tasks

Some objects must be left where they are.

“put everything except the eggplant on the plate”

Ordered 14 tasks

The placements must occur in a stated order.

“first put the banana in the bin, then put the orange in the bin”

Duplicates 8 tasks

Several identical objects, so the instruction refers to them by count, not by name.

“put both corn cobs on the cloth”

Task horizon scaling

Every system loses success as the task horizon grows. Orchestration slows the decline, and tool selection systems scale furthest.

Success falls with every subgoal

The low-level policy succeeds on 85.4%, 26.9% and 0.0% of tasks with 1, 2 and 5 subgoals. The Proprioceptive verifier keeps 58.4% at 2 subgoals and 8.3% at 5.

Tool selection scales furthest

On 5-subgoal tasks PSP-Agent still succeeds in 27.3% of episodes, against 11.1% for the Privileged verifier. Both use Gemini 3.7 Flash where they query a VLM.

Revisit is the hardest category

Revisit tasks send an object back to a destination it has left. The learned-policy systems reach 2.3% on average, GPT-Policy 43.5%.

Efficiency and cost

The most successful system is also by far the most expensive. Visual verification adds reliability at a small cost.

Success has a price

GPT-Policy costs $6.49 per successful episode and up to $8,134 for one full evaluation. PSP-Agent costs $0.33 per successful episode.

Verification is cheap

The Proprioceptive verifier queries the VLM only after the gripper opens, at $0.045 per episode.

A longer budget would help little

The Proprioceptive verifier completes 42.1% of all subgoals in the first half of the simulated time budget and 49.5% by its end.

Why subgoals fail

With verification, most lost subgoals come from physical execution, not from the orchestrator.

Verification prevents early advances

Without verification, 32.6–43.4% of subgoals fail because the orchestrator moves on before they are done. With the Proprioceptive verifier this drops to 3.4%.

Failed episodes stall early

Half of the failed episodes complete no subgoal after the first 28% of the budget. On 10-subgoal tasks, 31.0% of subgoals are never started.

TiPToP often finds no plan

TiPToP's planner finds no plan for 50.9% of its subgoals. PSP-Agent marks 16.2% of its subgoals as completed when they are not.

Offline VLM benchmark

VLMs decompose instructions accurately, but struggle with scene state tracking.

Plan decomposition is accurate

Gemini 3.7 Flash and GPT-5.6 Sol lead plan decomposition with 93.1% exact matches.

State tracking is the hardest

The best state tracking score is 70.8% (Gemini 3.7 Flash): the status of every subgoal must be right.

Gemini 3.7 Flash is the default

It has the highest mean over the four capabilities (80.8%). In simulation every VLM query uses it, except those of GPT-Policy (GPT-6 Astra).

Real-world experiments

On physical WidowX and Franka robots, the low-level policy collapses on long tasks and the ranking of the systems holds.

The low-level policy collapses

On multi-subgoal tasks it succeeds in 1.2% of episodes on the WidowX and 5.6% on the Franka.

Orchestration helps on hardware

The Proprioceptive verifier reaches 31.0% (WidowX) and 58.3% (Franka) on the same tasks, PSP-Agent 76.2% and 75.0%.

Simulation preserves the ranking

Both robots rank the three systems as in simulation: low-level policy, Proprioceptive verifier, PSP-Agent.

Policies, orchestrators and agents can all be evaluated on LADDER.

A policy

A low-level policy or any other end-to-end model. It runs as a small server and returns the next arm pose.

Example →

An orchestrator

A system that tells the low-level policy of each suite what to do next, in words. One Python class.

An agent

A system that moves the arm through tools, using depth and the camera calibration. One Python class.

  1. Start from the example for your kind of system and replace the model with yours.
  2. Run all tasks of the three suites, or the 10 tasks of LADDER-Lite for a quick first result.
  3. Open a pull request with your episode logs. We recompute every score from the logs.
